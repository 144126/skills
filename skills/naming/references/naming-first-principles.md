# Naming apps: root research

Question: what makes an app name work? Psychology peeled to first principles, for Claude to use when making or judging names.

Status: done (20 search turns, 25 sources). Making many names to choose from: [creativity-first-principles.md](https://github.com/144126/skills/blob/master/skills/creative/references/creativity-first-principles.md).

The rule I follow: [naming](https://github.com/144126/skills/blob/master/skills/naming/SKILL.md).

## Floor

A name is met before any evidence about the app. Later it is the cue people must pull up to find the app again. So it does two jobs, and two systems judge it.

- **Judgment:** with nothing else to go on, people read ease as a sign. Easy to process feels familiar, and familiar feels safe and good. A hard name makes the same thing seem newer and riskier.
- **Memory:** common words are easy to pull up. Rare and concrete words are easy to recognize and stick. What is easiest to like is not what is easiest to remember.

So naming is choosing a point on the line between easy and distinct, for the job the name must do. Then the sounds and the meaning must fit the promise, because both are read automatically.

Under it: liking for the familiar is learned. A thing met again and again, with no harm after it, comes to feel safe (Zajonc). Why minds treat the new as a possible threat is a question about evolution. Stop there.

## Layers peeled (shortest first)

1. Choose a simple name that is easy to say and spell, distinct from other apps, and hints at what the app does.
2. Why easy? Ease is read as familiarity, and familiarity as safety. Hard names made food additives seem more harmful and rides seem riskier. Easy names, and the people who carry them, are judged more kindly. Stocks with easy names did better in their first days of trading.
3. Why does ease feel good? Easy processing makes a small real smile, measured on the smiling muscle. Repetition with nothing bad after it builds liking by itself.
4. Why not as easy as possible? Ease buys liking, not memory. Easy slogans were liked more and remembered less. Common words are recalled better; rare words are recognized better. Brand names split the same way between recall and recognition.
5. Why do sounds matter? Sounds carry meaning on their own, automatically and across languages. Front vowels feel small, fast, sharp; back vowels feel big, slow, dull. People pick the name whose sound fits the product, about 2 to 1. Repeated sounds are liked more when heard aloud.
6. Why hint at the benefit? A name that states a benefit lifts recall of that benefit and lowers recall of other claims. The name locks the position.
7. Next "why" (why do minds treat the new as risky?) is about evolution. Stop.

## Lookalikes split

- **Liking vs remembering.** Different outcomes. The features that raise one lower the other.
- **Recall vs recognition.** Recall: pulling the name up unaided, to tell a friend or type into search. Recognition: knowing it on sight, in a store list or on a home screen. Common words help recall; rare words help recognition.
- **Sound meaning vs word meaning.** Sound symbolism works even in made-up words. Suggestiveness is what the word itself means.
- **Easy vs short.** The pronunciation effect held apart from name length.
- **Two accounts of familiarity.** The fluency camp says ease is mistaken for familiarity. Zajonc says mere exposure cannot be explained by fluency; it is conditioning by the absence of harm. Both predict the same thing: repeated without harm, liked more.
- **Name vs subtitle.** In the App Store, the name carries identity and the subtitle explains the value.

## When the rule flips

- **Special and premium:** difficulty makes a product feel rare and special, which raises its value. For everyday things, ease wins because it signals familiar and safe.
- **Thrill:** hard-to-say ride names read as more exciting and adventurous, not only riskier.
- **Over time:** the name pulls hardest before people know the thing. For single stocks it stopped predicting returns at 6 months and 1 year, though the most and least fluent baskets kept a gap.

## Where the evidence is thin

- Most studies use made-up names, students, and one exposure. No study found tracks names against real app success.
- Real words vs coined words: Lerman and Garbarino (2002) compared them. Only the abstract was readable. The often-quoted "68.8% vs 38.1% recall" comes from an AI-written summary on academia.edu and from a blog, not from a readable page of the paper. Left out.
- Lowrey, Shrum and Dubitsky (2003) on spelling, rhyme, and familiarity: abstract blocked. Only what Shrum and Lowrey restate in their chapter is used.
- Hard first consonants (k, g, b, p, t, d) help recall of nonsense words, but the chapter says the gain may come from odd-looking letters, not from the sound.
- Unusual spelling helps memory, but Apple says easy to spell. No study found tests typing a name after hearing it once. The "hear it once, type it" test is my inference from the recall job.
- A common word taken from a far domain (easy to recall, still distinct in its category) is my inference, not tested.
- Hodges found slogans with less "perceptually distinct" words were remembered more. The measure is about single words, on slogans, not names. Not used in the rule.

## Evidence (pinned)

### Ease reads as familiar, safe, and good

> Hard-to-say names make a thing seem new, and so riskier. — https://people.uncw.edu/tothj/PSY510/Song-DisFluency%20and%20Risk-PS-2009.pdf §Abstract (Song & Schwarz 2009) — "Low processing fluency fosters the impression that a stimulus is unfamiliar, which in turn results in perceptions of higher risk, independent of whether the risk is desirable or undesirable."

> Food additives with hard names were rated more harmful. — same URL §Abstract — "ostensible food additives were rated as more harmful when their names were difficult to pronounce than when their names were easy to pronounce"

> Rides with hard names seemed more likely to make you sick, and more exciting. — same URL §Abstract — "amusement-park rides were rated as more likely to make one sick (an undesirable risk) and also as more exciting and adventurous (a desirable risk) when their names were difficult to pronounce"

> People mistake ease for familiarity. — same URL §Introduction — "people (erroneously) infer familiarity from ease of processing"

> Zajonc's account: the new is uncertain, the familiar is safe. — same URL §Introduction — "novel stimuli are associated with uncertainty, whereas familiar stimuli are considered safe, at least in the absence of negative memories"

> Easy names, and the people who carry them, are judged more positively (5 studies). — https://ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Laham2012TNPEW.pdf §Abstract (Laham, Koval & Alter 2012) — "easy-to-pronounce names (and their bearers) are judged more positively than difficult-to-pronounce names"

> It held apart from length, oddness, and foreignness. — same URL §Abstract — "These effects obtain independent of name length, unusualness, typicality, foreignness, and orthographic regularity."

> Real world: lawyers with easier surnames held higher posts. — same URL §Abstract — "people with easier-to-pronounce surnames occupy higher status positions in law firms"

> Stocks with fluent names beat disfluent ones in the short term. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1482615/ §Abstract (Alter & Oppenheimer 2006) — "an initial investment of $1,000 yielded a profit of $112 more after 1 day of trading for a basket of fluently named shares than for a basket of disfluently named shares"

> For single stocks the effect faded. — same URL §Results — "the complexity of a share’s name did not reliably predict its performance at 6 months [β = −0.05, t (n = 89) = −0.47, P = 0.64] or 1 year"

> The extreme baskets kept a gap. — same URL §Results — "$112 after 1 day, $118 after 1 week, $277 after 6 months (all Ps < 0.05), and $333 after 1 year (P < 0.10)"

> Easy processing makes a small smile. — https://www.facialemg.com/wp-content/uploads/MindAtEase.pdf §Abstract (Winkielman & Cacioppo 2001) — "easy-to-process pictures elicited higher activity over the region of zygomaticus major, indicating positive affect"

> Mere exposure works like conditioning by the absence of harm. — https://colab.ws/articles/10.1111%2F1467-8721.00154 §Abstract (Zajonc 2001, doi:10.1111/1467-8721.00154) — "The repeated-exposure paradigm can be regarded as a form of classical conditioning if we assume that the absence of aversive events constitutes the unconditioned stimulus."

> Zajonc rejects fluency as its cause. — same URL §Abstract — "a robust phenomenon that cannot be explained by an appeal to recognition memory or perceptual fluency"

### Liking and memory pull apart

> Fluent wording is liked more and remembered less (800+ slogans, lab, eye tracking, field). — https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/30412/3/Hodges%20Estes%20Warren%20PREPRINT.pdf §Abstract (Hodges, Estes & Warren 2024) — "linguistic properties that make a slogan easier to process (i.e., more fluent) result in slogans that are more likable but less memorable"

> Liked more: shorter, no brand name, common and abstract words. — same URL §Abstract — "participants indicated a more favorable attitude towards slogans that are shorter, omit the brand name, and use words that are linguistically frequent, perceptually distinct, and abstract."

> Remembered more: longer, brand name included, rare and concrete words. — same URL §Abstract — "participants were more likely to remember slogans that are longer, include the brand name, and use words that are linguistically infrequent, concrete, and less perceptually distinct."

> Familiarity alone explained almost half of liking. — same URL §Study 1 Results — "Familiarity explained a large and significant percentage of the variance in ratings of slogan liking, F(1, 818) = 692.77, p < .001, R2 = .46"

> Common words are recalled better; rare words are recognized better. — https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03197623 §Abstract (Mandler 1982) — "High-frequency words are recalled better than are low-frequency words, but low-frequency words produce higher hit rates in a recognition test than do high-frequency words."

> Brand names: word vs nonword memory depends on the test. — https://www.academia.edu/52750419/Recall_and_recognition_of_brand_names_A_comparison_of_word_and_nonword_name_types §Abstract (Lerman & Garbarino 2002) — "memory for these brand name types depends on whether it is accessed via recall or recognition"

### Sounds carry meaning

> The sound of a name tells size, speed, strength, weight. — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1008184423824 §Abstract (Klink 2000) — "Results of two studies indicate that the sound of a brand name can communicate information about the product, e.g. its size, speed, strength, weight, etc."

> It happens automatically. — https://linguist62n.stanford.edu/yorkston.pdf §Abstract (Yorkston & Menon 2004) — "the manner in which phonetic effects of brand names manifest is automatic in as much as it is uncontrollable, outside awareness and effortless"

> Front vowels: small, fast, sharp. Back vowels: big, slow, dull. — https://coehuman.uodiyala.edu.iq/uploads/Coehuman%20library%20pdf/English%20library%D9%83%D8%AA%D8%A8%20%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A7%D9%86%D9%83%D9%84%D9%8A%D8%B2%D9%8A/linguistics/LowreyBookChapter2006.Final.pdf §Phonetic Symbolism and Perceptions of Product Attributes (Shrum & Lowrey) — "front vowel sounds tend to be perceived as smaller, faster, sharper, whereas back vowel sounds tend to be perceived as bigger, slower, duller"

> Front vowel example: the "i" in mill. Back: the "a" in mall. — https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/09/070912130815.htm (Lowrey & Shrum 2007) — "Front vowel sounds are ones that are made with the tongue forward in the mouth, such as the sound of the letter "I" in mill."

> People pick the name whose sound fits the product, about 2 to 1. — same URL — "participants preferred words with front vowel sounds when the product category was a convertible or a knife (by about a 2:1 margin), but preferred words with back vowel sounds when the product category was an SUV or hammer"

> The "yoo" of putrid lost in every category. — same URL — "Regardless of product category, words this vowel sound were least preferred by consumers."

> Sound-shape links hold across 25 languages. — https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC8591387 §Abstract (Ćwiek et al. 2022) — "tested the bouba/kiki effect across speakers of 25 languages representing nine language families and 10 writing systems. Overall, we found strong evidence for the effect across languages"

> Repeated sounds are liked more when spoken aloud. — https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2010/10/21/Repetitive-sounds-could-hold-secret-of-successful-brands/ (Argo, Popa & Smith 2010, quoted) — "The findings suggest that brand names containing phonetic sound repetition, if spoken aloud, are evaluated more favorably than those that do not contain such repetition or that are merely read silently."

> Limit: repetition that breaks sound rules loses. — same URL — "consonants could be overused in a word like ‘ranthfanth’ which they found was less favorable to consumers than ‘rantifanti’"

### Memory devices

> Levers for meaning and distinctiveness. — https://coehuman.uodiyala.edu.iq/uploads/Coehuman%20library%20pdf/English%20library%D9%83%D8%AA%D8%A8%20%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A7%D9%86%D9%83%D9%84%D9%8A%D8%B2%D9%8A/linguistics/LowreyBookChapter2006.Final.pdf §Introduction (Shrum & Lowrey) — "phonetic devices such as rhyming, vowel repetition, and alliteration, orthographic devices such as unusual spellings or abbreviations, and morphological devices such as the compounding or blending of words"

> Hard first consonants help recall of nonsense words. — same URL — "nonsense words beginning with stop consonants (also called “initial plosives”) are better recalled than words that do not start with initial stop consonants"

> Limit: it may be the look of the letter, not the sound. — same URL — "It may be that a letter such as k simply looks funny, either because of its odd, angular shape, or simply because it doesn’t occur that often in the English language."

> Unusual spelling helps recall and recognition. — same URL — "unusual spellings (e.g,. Kool-Aid), which have been shown to enhance brand name recall and recognition (Lowrey et al., 2003)"

> Fit with the category helps recall and preference. — same URL — "the congruency or “fit” between a brand name and the product category can increase important marketing variables such as recall, preference, and inference"

### Meaning locks the position

> A benefit name lifts recall of that benefit. — https://colab.ws/articles/10.1177%2F002224299806200105 §Abstract (Keller, Heckler & Houston 1998, doi:10.1177/002224299806200105) — "a brand name explicitly conveying a product benefit (e.g., PicturePerfect televisions) leads to higher recall of an advertised benefit claim consistent in meaning with the brand name"

> And lowers recall of unrelated claims. — same URL §Abstract — "a suggestive brand name leads to lower recall of a subsequently advertised benefit claim unrelated in product meaning (e.g., superior sound)"

> It makes later repositioning harder. — https://www.msi.org/working-papers/the-effects-of-brand-name-suggestiveness-on-advertising-recall/ — "although a suggestive brand name may facilitate initial brand positioning, it may hamper subsequent efforts to reposition the brand in new, unrelated directions"

### When difficulty helps

> Special-occasion goods: ease makes them feel common. — https://spinup-000d1a-wp-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com/faculty/wp-content/uploads/sites/48/2019/06/MakingProductsFeelSpecial_WhenMetacognitiveDifficultyEnhancesEvaluation_000.pdf §Abstract (Pocheptsova, Labroo & Dhar, working paper of JMR 2010) — "in the context of special occasion high-end goods, higher fluency which indicates abundance of the product is a negative cue because it makes the products feel less special"

> Everyday goods: ease signals familiar and safe. — same URL §Abstract — "In the context of everyday objects, increased fluency is a positive cue that the product is familiar and safe"

### App Store

> Apple's own rule. — https://developer.apple.com/app-store/product-page/ §App name — "Choose a simple, memorable name that is easy to spell and hints at what your app does. Be distinctive. Avoid names that use generic terms or are too similar to existing app names."

> Length cap. — same URL §App name — "An app name can be up to 30 characters long."

> Let the subtitle explain. — same URL §Subtitle — "Consider using this, rather than your app’s name, to explain the value of your app in greater detail."
