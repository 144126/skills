# How to be creative: root research

Question: how do you come up with ideas that are new, that work, and that feel beautiful? Peeled to first principles, for Claude to use on every task that can gain from it.

Status: partial. The usage limit cut the research short. Floor, layers, methods, and beauty are pinned. Not yet read: LLM prompting studies (Si 2024, Meincke 2024, temperature, writing cliches).

The rule I follow: [creative](https://github.com/144126/skills/blob/master/skills/creative/SKILL.md).

## Floor

A mind is a prediction machine (it guesses what comes next from what it has seen before).

- **Making:** it serves up the most likely thing first. So first ideas are typical.
- **Enjoying:** it likes best the thing that breaks its guess and then lets it build a better guess (learning progress).

So a creative, beautiful idea is one the audience could not predict, but "gets" right after. Surprising, then inevitable. You cannot know such an idea's worth in advance, because if you could, it would not be new to you. The only way to find it is to make many varied tries away from your own defaults, then pick with a model of the audience.

For me (an AI): I am trained to predict the most probable next word. Tuning after that makes it worse. My first idea is the average of what people wrote. The audience already predicts the average, so it gives them nothing to learn.

## Layers peeled (shortest first)

1. Make many tries. Keep the ones that are new, work, and surprise.
2. Why many tries? You cannot know a new idea's value before you try it. The idea you can fully foresee is routine, not creative.
3. Why is the first idea typical? Memory hands out first what was most often needed before. Common links get used up, then rarer ones come.
4. Why does "new but makes sense" feel good? Pleasure peaks when a surprise can be resolved. Too plain or too random both bore.
5. Why does resolving feel good? Minds reward learning progress. Babies look away from what is too simple and from what is too complex.
6. Next "why" (why did evolution build a reward for learning?) is a different question. Stop.

## Lookalikes split

- **Making vs picking.** Making ideas (variation) and choosing ideas (selection) are different jobs. Both matter.
- **Creative vs beautiful.** Creative = new + works + surprise. Beautiful = a felt reward in the person who meets it. An idea can be one without the other.
- **New to me vs new to the world.** New-to-me is enough to count as creative thinking.

## Evidence (pinned)

### What creativity is

> Creativity needs two things: new and working. — https://ead.ufpa.br/pluginfile.php/542154/mod_folder/content/0/Leituras%20Complementares%20n%C3%A3o%20acess%C3%ADveis/RUNCO%2C%20Mark%20A.%3B%20JAEGER%2C%20Garrett%20J.%20The%20standard%20definition%20of%20creativity.%20.pdf?forcedownload=1 §The Standard Definition — "The standard deﬁnition is bipartite: Creativity requires both originality and effectiveness."

> Random output is new but useless. — same URL §The Standard Definition — "A truly random process will often generate something that is merely original."

> A third test is surprise, and the three multiply (a zero on one makes the whole zero). — https://creativity.psu.edu/project/taking-the-u-s-patent-office-criteria-seriously/ §Description — "The modiﬁed version uses the criteria of novelty, utility, and surprise. Moreover, creativity assessments based on these three criteria are quantitative and multiplicative rather than qualitative or additive."

> New-to-me counts. — https://www.interaliamag.org/articles/margaret-boden-creativity-in-a-nutshell/ §intro — "P-creativity involves coming up with a surprising, valuable idea that's new to the person who comes up with it."

> Three kinds: combine, explore a style, transform a style. — same URL §The Three Forms of Creativity — "For creativity can happen in three main ways, which correspond to the three sorts of surprise."

> Combining: familiar parts, new pair. — same URL §The Three Forms of Creativity — "The first involves making unfamiliar combinations of familiar ideas. Examples include poetic imagery, collage in painting or textile art, and analogies."

> A combination only counts if a real link joins the parts. — same URL §The Three Forms of Creativity — "But the ideas/marbles have to have some intelligible conceptual pathway between them for the combination to "make sense.""

> Combining needs a big, varied store of knowledge. — same URL §The Three Forms of Creativity — "making — and also appreciating — the novel combination requires a rich store of knowledge in the person's mind, and many different ways of moving around within it."

> Transforming: change a rule of the style, so an impossible thought becomes possible. — same URL §Exploring Conceptual Spaces — "The supposedly impossible idea can come about only if the creator changes the pre-existing style in some way."

### Layer 2: you cannot foresee a new idea's value

> The fully foreseeable idea is the first thing that comes to mind, and it is routine. — https://simonton.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/243/2022/11/2022BVSRupdateCRJerrata.pdf §Sighted variations — "A variation of this kind cannot possibly be creative, for it represents routine or habitual thinking. It is literally the first thing that comes to mind"

> Creativity = (1 − probability) × usefulness × (1 − prior knowledge of that usefulness). — same URL §formal derivation — "c = (1 – p)u(1 – v), again 0 ≤ c ≤ 1."

> So every creative idea needs some blind trying. — same URL §Blind variations — "he would have been able to prove that all creative variations must entail a substantial amount of blindness."

> If you cannot know the value, you must test it. — same URL §Blind variations — "If the utility is unknown, it must be determined."

> More ideas, more good ideas (lab study, 246 people). — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4479710/ §Discussion — "We found that quantity was associated with quality on measures of divergent thinking customarily associated with creative cognition."

> Over a career the hit rate stays flat, so the busiest years hold the best work. — http://www.chrest.info/Fribourg_Cours_Expertise/Articles-www/IV%20Expertise%20et%20societe/Creativity%20as%20blind%20variation%20-%20target-Simonton.pdf §quality–quantity — "Those periods in which the creator produces the most total works will be those in which the most outstanding works appear, including the single best contribution"

### Layer 3: why the first idea is typical

> Memory serves first what was most often and most recently needed, because that is what is most likely needed again. — https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~paritosh/papers/KIP/AndersonSchooler1991ReflectionsOfEnvironmentOnMemory.pdf §Introduction — "It would be behaving optimally if it made most available those items that were most likely to be needed."

> Memory is shaped to match the world's statistics. — same URL §Abstract — "human memory has the form it does because it is adapted to these environmental relationships."

> When people invent, they start from the examples that come to mind fastest, even when told to be creative. — https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03195281 §Abstract — "This result held even when task constraints weighed against the use of such items (Experiment 2) and when participants were encouraged to be as creative as possible (Experiment 4)"

> Later ideas are better: one of the oldest findings in the field. — https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2012-22624-001 §Abstract — "The serial order effect—the tendency for later responses to a divergent thinking task to be better than earlier ones—is one of the oldest and most robust findings in modern creativity work."

> Why: first come the common links, later the rare ones. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7443938/ §Introduction — "After working on the problem for a period of time, people begin to draw on less common associations and less obvious approaches and, ultimately, arrive at more creative ideas."

> People wrongly expect to run dry, so they quit early and do worse (8 studies). — same URL §Significance — "Eight studies found that people expect their creativity to decline across an ideation session when it, in fact, tends to improve or persist"

> Limit: across 5 separate days, real creativity did drop. — same URL §Study 5 — "Inconsistent with prior literature, we found that actual creativity declined across the study as well"

### Layer 4: resolvable surprise feels good

> Music (80,000 chords, 745 pop songs): a surprise in a clear context pleases, and so does a sure thing in an unclear context. — https://www.stefan-koelsch.de/papers/cheung_harrison_meyer_pearce_haynes_koelsch_2019_uncertainty_and_surprise_jointly_predict_musical_pleasure_and_amygdala_hippocampus_and_auditory_cortex_activity_current_biology_29.pdf §Summary — "chords elicited high pleasure ratings when they deviated substantially from what the listener had expected (low uncertainty, high surprise) or, conversely, when they conformed to expectations in an uninformative context"

> Liking peaks at middle complexity. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6867811/ §Abstract — "indicating reliable preferences for music of intermediate complexity."

> The reward is learning: fixing wrong guesses and confirming right ones. — same URL §Abstract — "These results are consistent with theories that emphasize the intrinsic reward of learning, both by updating inaccurate predictions and validating accurate ones."

### Layer 4 more: resolvable surprise, in other fields

> Aristotle: the best plots surprise you, yet each event causes the next. — https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1974/pg1974.txt §Poetics IX — "Such an effect is best produced when the events come on us by surprise; and the effect is heightened when, at the same time, they follow as cause and effect."

> Easy processing feels good. — https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkiel/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf §Abstract — "The more fluently perceivers can process an object, the more positive their aesthetic response."

> Ease that comes as a surprise feels best ("unity in variety"). — same URL §Expectations and Attributions — "when processing is expected to be difficult, yet turns out to be easy, it creates a particularly strong experience of aesthetic pleasure"

> Products: people like new AND typical; each hides the other's effect. — https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:12648393%20AND%20SRC:MED&resultType=core&format=json §Abstract (Hekkert 2003) — "people prefer novel designs as long as the novelty does not affect typicality"

> Phrases: a twist that keeps the old phrase recoverable pleases most (6 experiments). — https://www.tau.ac.il/~giorar/files/Weapons%20of%20mass%20destruction2004.pdf §Abstract — "an optimally innovative stimulus, such that induces a novel response while allowing for the recovery of a salient one"

> Hits: songs too similar to their peers fail; some difference wins (~27,000 songs). — https://gwern.net/doc/culture/2017-askin.pdf §Abstract — "songs sounding too much like previous and contemporaneous productions—those that are highly typical—are less likely to succeed."

> Pictures: liking jumps right after you "get" a hidden face. — http://www.experimental-psychology.de/ccc/docs/pubs/MuthCarbon2013.pdf §Abstract — "Having an insight as well as the intensity of the insight predicted subsequent gains in liking."

> Humor: something wrong that is also OK, at the same moment. — https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:20587696%20AND%20SRC:MED&resultType=core&format=json §Abstract (McGraw 2010) — "laughter and amusement result from violations that are simultaneously seen as benign."

> Limit: "middle complexity is best" is partly an average of two kinds of people (30 people). — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2016.00112/full §Results — "one group of participants in our sample had increasingly lower liking ratings for increasingly more complex stimuli, while a second group of participants had increasingly higher liking ratings"

### Layer 5: minds reward learning progress

> Babies look away from what is too simple and from what is too complex. — https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0036399 §Abstract — "Infants' probability of looking away was greatest on stimulus items whose complexity (negative log probability) according to the model was either very low or very high."

> Why: known stuff and unknowable stuff both waste effort. — same URL §Introduction — "Failure to be selective would waste precious computational resources on material that is already known (too simple) or unknowable (too complex)."

> Babies (43) follow learning progress most of all. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7531891/ §Results — "strongest predictor of infants' looking away was the learning progress offered by the stimulus"

> Surprise and predictability alone mattered far less. — same URL §Results — "these effects were much weaker (five and three times, respectively) than the impact that learning progress"

> Theory (not a test): interesting = the speed at which your model of it improves. Beauty = how simple it has become. — https://arxiv.org/pdf/0812.4360 §Abstract — "This drive maximizes interestingness, the first derivative of subjective beauty or compressibility, that is, the steepness of the learning curve."

> Theory: pure sameness and pure noise are both boring. — same URL §2.6 — "Neither the arbitrary nor the fully predictable is truly novel or surprising—only data with still unknown algorithmic regularities are"

### What works: making

> Tell yourself to be original. You get fewer ideas, but better ones. — https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.psu.edu/dist/c/122043/files/2020/08/2014b_Nusbaum_PACA.pdf §Abstract — "asking people to be creative yielded better but fewer responses, suggesting a focus on quality over quantity."

> Some limits help and too many hurt: an upside-down U. — https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/20459/1/Acar,%20Tarakci%20and%20van%20Knippenberg%20(in%20press)_Creativity%20and%20Innovation%20under%20Constraints.pdf §Introduction — "suggests an inverted U-shaped effect of constraints on creativity and innovation following motivational, cognitive, and social mechanisms."

> Limits block the most obvious answer. — same URL §Output constraints — "output constraints in the design process reduce the tendency to suggest only the most obvious solutions"

> People copy an example they are shown, even its flaws (engineers: cords in 36% of designs without the example, 78% with it, Table 4). — https://cecas.clemson.edu/cedar/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/9-JanssonAndSmith1991.pdf §Discussion — "both students and professional engineers showed the same effects"

> Far-off inspiration hurts while ideas are flowing, so save it for when you are stuck (245 people). — https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/chan17semantically.pdf §Abstract — "Receiving far ideas during productive ideation resulted in slower ideation and less within-category exploration, without significant benefits for novelty"

> Two roads to originality: many categories, or one category in great depth. — https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/the-dual-pathway-to-creativity-model-creative-ideation-as-a-funct/ §Abstract — "originality can also be achieved by exploring a few content categories in great depth (i.e., persistence)"

> Being stuck breaks when you drop a rule you made up yourself, or split a chunk into parts. — https://somby.ceu.edu/sites/somby.ceu.edu/files/attachment/basicpage/6/1999knoblich.pdf §Abstract — "the relaxation of constraints on the solution and the decomposition of perceptual chunks."

> Past experience both helps and blocks. — same URL §Abstract — "Overcoming impasses in insight is a special case of the general need to override the imperatives of past experience in the face of novel conditions."

> Setting a problem aside helps, most of all on open-ended tasks. — https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/2009-sio.pdf §Abstract — "The authors identified a positive incubation effect, with divergent thinking tasks benefiting more than linguistic and visual insight tasks from incubation."

> Training works (70 studies, effect 0.68). Finding the real problem is its strongest part. — http://www.psychologie.uni-mannheim.de/cip/tut/seminare_wittmann/meta_fribourg/sources/Scott_etal_2004_Effectiveness_of_creativity_training.pdf §Results — "problem identification (β = .48), idea generation (β = .18), and conceptual combination (β = .14) made the strongest unique contributions to training effects."

> Art students who explored longer before drawing made more original work. (This is a secondary source, not the book.) — https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/reading-the-mind/202604/the-1960s-art-school-experiment-that-redefined-creativity — "the problem-finding artists produced the more original and aesthetically compelling work"

> Award-winning ads follow a few patterns. — https://faculty.runi.ac.il/jgoldenberg/pdf/sparks.pdf §Templates — "89% of the award-winning ads match as few as six major templates"

> Top science is a very conventional base with one unusual combination (17.9 million papers, twice as likely to be a hit). — https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/uzzi/htm/papers/science-2013-uzzi-468-72.pdf §Abstract — "The highest-impact science is primarily grounded in exceptionally conventional combinations of prior work yet simultaneously features an intrusion of unusual combinations."

> Chasing a big goal head-on can trap you. Novelty finds the stepping stones. — https://gwern.net/doc/reinforcement-learning/exploration/2011-lehman.pdf §Introduction — "the more ambitious the goal, the more difficult it may be to articulate an appropriate objective function and the more likely it is that search can be deceived by local optima"

> Brainstorming in a group gives fewer and worse ideas than the same people working alone. — https://dynamic.decorrespondent.nl/downloads/michiel-de-hoog/Mullen-1991-Productivity-Loss-in-Brainstorming-Groups.pdf §Abstract — "brainstorming groups are significantly less productive than nominal groups, in terms of both quantity and quality."

> Creative brains run networks together that usually work against each other (163 people). — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5798342 §Abstract — "highly creative people are characterized by the ability to simultaneously engage these large-scale brain networks"

> Naming 10 words as unlike each other as possible tracks creativity (8,914 people). — https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1073/pnas.2022340118&resultType=core&format=json §Abstract — "we asked 8,914 participants to name 10 words that are as different from each other as possible."

### What works: picking

> People pick the doable idea over the original one. — https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:19267959%20AND%20SRC:MED&resultType=core&format=json §Abstract — "We identified the strong tendency of our participants to select feasible and desirable ideas, at the cost of originality"

> Told to pick the original ones, they do, but it feels worse. — same URL — "explicitly instructing participants to select creative or original ideas did improve selection effectiveness with regard to idea originality"

> Makers judge other people's ideas well and their own badly (339 circus pros, 13,248 audience members). — https://justinmberg.com/wp-content/uploads/Berg_2016_ASQ.pdf §General Discussion — "But creators overvalued their own ideas"

> People who only judge undervalue new ideas. — same URL §Theory — "managers may undervalue novel ideas and overvalue conventional ideas"

> Several drafts side by side before feedback beat one draft improved in a line: they score better and vary more. — https://hci.stanford.edu/publications/2010/parallel-prototyping/ParallelPrototyping2010.pdf §Abstract — "ads created in the Parallel condition significantly outperformed those from the Serial condition. Moreover, independent raters found Parallel prototypes to be more diverse."

### Human-only levers (they do not apply to me)

> Dozing off for about a minute tripled insight (83% vs 30%, 103 people). — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8654287/ §Abstract — "A single minute of sleep onset is sufficient to boost the probability of insight by a factor of three compared to wake."

> Happy, energetic moods help a little. Fear and anxiety hurt (102 effect sizes). — https://www.uva.nl/binaries/content/documents/personalpages/b/a/m.baas/profile-en/profile-en/uva:foldout/cpitem%5B2%5D/asset §Abstract — "Creativity is enhanced most by positive mood states that are activating and associated with an approach motivation and promotion focus (e.g., happiness)"

### For me: why a language model drifts to the bland middle

> Picking the most probable words makes text bland and loopy. — https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.09751 §Abstract — "maximization-based decoding methods such as beam search lead to degeneration — output text that is bland, incoherent, or gets stuck in repetitive loops."

> Human text is less probable than model text, because people avoid stating the obvious. — same URL §Natural language does not maximize probability — "Natural language rarely remains in a high probability zone for multiple consecutive time steps, instead veering into lower-probability but more informative tokens."

> Word-by-word likelihood training pulls toward the lowest common denominator. — same URL §Natural language does not maximize probability — "such models are forced to favor the lowest common denominator, rather than informative language."

> Tuning on human ratings makes it worse: raters pick familiar text. — https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01171 §Abstract — "typicality bias in preference data, whereby annotators systematically favor familiar text as a result of well-established findings in cognitive psychology"

> One model repeats itself; different models give near-identical answers (26K open questions). — https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22954 §Abstract — "different models produce strikingly similar outputs"

> 2026: the pull to the same answer is already in pretraining; tuning only reveals and boosts it. — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.11426 §Abstract — "We conclude that convergence can be amplified, but not introduced."

> A tested fix: ask for several answers, each with a probability. — https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01171 §Abstract — "in creative writing, VS increases diversity by 1.6-2.1x over direct prompting"

> Stronger models gain more from that fix. — same URL §Abstract — "an emergent trend that more capable models benefit more from VS"
