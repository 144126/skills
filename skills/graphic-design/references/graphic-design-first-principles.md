# Aesthetically pleasing graphic design: root research

Question: what is the root of graphic design that looks beautiful, feels professional, and sticks in memory, with the focus on beauty? Science, math, and psychology, peeled to first principles, for Claude to use when designing.

Status: done (50 search turns, 41 sources).

The rule I follow: [graphic-design](https://github.com/144126/skills/blob/master/skills/graphic-design/SKILL.md).

## Answer to the question behind the question

"If it is aesthetically pleasing, it is memorable and professional." Half true.

- **Professional: yes.** Beauty spills over (a halo effect). The same content in a better design is judged more credible, more usable, and even more true.
- **Memorable: no.** Beautiful, peaceful images are often forgotten. Memory needs a meaningful anchor: a person, a face, a recognizable object, or one element that differs from everything around it.

So a design needs two things: beauty (for liking and trust) plus one distinct, meaningful anchor (for memory).

## Floor

The visual system is a compressor tuned to nature. It learned to code natural scenes with as little effort as possible. It groups parts the way edges in nature belong together.

- What it can code cheaply feels good: clear groups, symmetry or balance, few parts, a typical layout, legible type, and natural fine-to-coarse structure. Psychologists call this fluency (ease of processing).
- It feels best when a small surprise resolves into a better prediction. Too plain gives no reward; too busy gives no resolution.

Next "why" (why did the brain evolve to save effort?) is a different question (energy and evolution). Stop.

## Layers peeled (shortest first)

1. Keep it simple, ordered, and typical for its kind. Add one twist and one memorable anchor.
2. Why simple? Busy designs cost effort. Low complexity plus a typical look is judged most appealing within 17–50 ms. Too busy hurts far more than too plain.
3. Why do grouping, symmetry, and alignment look good? They are how the eye codes efficiently. Grouping rules come from natural edge statistics. Symmetry is detected fast and was the top predictor of beauty for abstract patterns.
4. Why does efficient coding feel good? Ease of processing feels positive. The reward is the move from uncertainty to a better prediction.
5. Why is the eye built that way? The first visual cells match a sparse code learned from natural images. Art, comics, and manga copy natural image statistics.
6. Next "why" changes the subject. Stop.

## Lookalikes split

- **Pleasing vs professional vs memorable.** Pleasing and professional share one root (fluency plus the halo). Memorable has another root (meaning plus distinctiveness).
- **Preference vs performance.** People prefer 50–70 characters per line but read longer screen lines faster. People prefer some fonts that are not their fastest. Design for the goal.
- **Complexity vs density.** One study found appealing homepages dense with bold graphics. Complexity (disorder and clutter) hurts; rich but well-organized content can please.
- **Shared vs personal taste.** People agree on faces and landscapes, less on designed things. The ideal colorfulness and complexity vary by group.

## Where the evidence is thin

- The "art matches natural statistics" model (Redies) is a theory backed by correlations, not causal tests.
- The golden ratio and the rule of thirds are weak; see [aesthetics-motion-color-shape.md](aesthetics-motion-color-shape.md).
- "White space raises comprehension 20%" is a secondary-citation myth. The real margin study is small (Chaparro 2004).
- F-pattern reading comes from industry eye-tracking (Nielsen Norman Group), not peer review.
- Typeface impressions (Henderson 2004) and "blue reads competent" (Labrecque 2012): pages blocked, so they are left out.

## Evidence (pinned)

### Floor: the eye is built to code nature cheaply

> A code that uses as few active cells as possible, learned from natural images, reproduces the brain's first visual cells. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=8637596,18073053,11248261&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract (Olshausen & Field 1996) — "a learning algorithm that attempts to find sparse linear codes for natural scenes will develop a complete family of localized, oriented, bandpass receptive fields"

> Why: it is more efficient for later stages. — same URL — "The resulting sparse image code provides a more efficient representation for later stages of processing"

> The brain's grouping rules come from the statistics of edges in nature. — https://www.cns.nyu.edu/csh/csh06/PDFs/VR_2001_Edge%20co-occuren.pdf §Abstract — "it is these statistics that drove the evolution of the grouping mechanisms."

> A grouping rule taken straight from natural edge statistics predicts how well people see contours. — same URL §Abstract — "contour detection performance is quantitatively predicted by a local grouping rule derived directly from the co-occurrence statistics"

> Model (not a test): art pleases by matching the natural statistics the eye is tuned to. — same eutils URL §Abstract (Redies 2007) — "it is proposed that an artist creates a work of art so that it induces a specific resonant state in the visual system. This resonant state is thought to be based on the adaptation of the visual system to natural scenes."

> Art, cartoons, comics, and manga share the natural 1/f² spectrum (fine detail fading smoothly into coarse). — https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0012268 §Results — "the radially averaged spectral amplitudes in art images fall according to a power-law with increasing spatial frequency, similar to 1/f noise"

> Pleasure = moving from uncertainty to a better prediction. Artists often break a prediction they set up first. — https://sandervandecruys.be/pdf/2011_VandeCruys-i-Perception_Putting_reward.pdf §Abstract — "The ensuing rewarding effect is derived from this transition from a state of uncertainty to a state of increased predictability."

### Complexity and typicality

> Judged complexity tracks compressed file size (how much a file shrinks). Form complexity and form probability are two sides of one thing. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=16435958&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract (Donderi 2006) — "Form complexity and form probability are shown to be linked through their reciprocal relationship in complexity theory"

> Websites with low complexity and a typical look for their kind were rated most appealing. — https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/ §Abstract (Tuch 2012) — "Overall, websites with low VC and high PT were perceived as highly appealing."

> This is judged in 17–50 ms. — same URL — "VC and PT affect aesthetic perception even within 17ms, though the effect of PT is less pronounced than the one of VC."

> Complexity matters more than colorfulness; too busy hurts far more than too plain (2.4 million ratings, nearly 40,000 people). — https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2014/reinecke14visual.pdf §General Results — "visual complexity plays a more important role as a predictor of appeal than colorfulness."

> The ideal level varies by group: women liked more color, more education lowered it, Russians preferred less complexity. — same URL §Abstract — "the level of colorfulness and visual complexity at which visual appeal is highest strongly varies"

> Complexity and colorfulness models explain about half of first-impression appeal at 500 ms (450 sites, 548 people). — https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120378-cc85-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b §Abstract (Reinecke 2013) — "these models explain approximately half of the variance in the ratings of aesthetic appeal given after viewing a website for 500ms only."

> People agree on faces and landscapes; they disagree about art and architecture. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=29936343&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract — "preferences for images of faces and landscapes containing a high proportion of shared taste, while preferences for images of exterior architecture, interior architecture and artworks reflected strong individual differences."

### Beauty drives "professional"

> First impressions of a website form in 50 ms and hold. — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4863498/ §Review (citing Lindgaard 2006) — "The first impression of a website is formed within 50ms and is highly stable."

> Credibility: "design and look" was the top factor people mentioned (2,500+ people). — same URL (citing Fogg 2003) — "The largest category, 'design and look', was indicated by 46.1% of the respondents."

> Same content, better design, higher credibility. — https://www.makinggood.ac.nz/media/1276/robinsholmes_2008_aestheticsandcredibilityinwebdesign.pdf §Abstract — "Given the same content, a higher aesthetic treatment will increase perceived credibility."

> Beauty sets judged usability even after use; real usability did not (ATM study). — https://academic.oup.com/iwc/article-abstract/13/2/127/898608 §Abstract (Tractinsky 2000) — "the degree of system's aesthetics affected the post-use perceptions of both aesthetics and usability, whereas the degree of actual usability had no such effect."

### Memorable is not the same as beautiful

> Beautiful and unusual photos are not the memorable ones; rooms with people and visible faces are (2,222 photos). — https://web.mit.edu/phillipi/www/publications/UnderstandingMemorability.pdf §Abstract — "Contrary to popular belief, unusual or aesthetically pleasing scenes do not tend to be highly memorable."

> What sticks: enclosed spaces with people and faces; what fades: vistas and peaceful scenes. — same URL §Abstract — "images of enclosed spaces containing people with visible faces are memorable, while images of vistas and peaceful scenes are not."

> Memorability is a stable, shared property of an image. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=26353315&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract (Isola 2014) — "memorability is an intrinsic and stable property of an image that is shared across different viewers, and remains stable across delays."

> Charts: color and a recognizable object (person, cartoon, logo) raise memorability; common chart types are less memorable (2,070 charts). — http://olivalab.mit.edu/Papers/Borkin_etal_MemorableVisualization_TVCG2013.pdf §Abstract — "attributes like color and the inclusion of a human recognizable object enhance memorability"

> Picture memory is almost limitless, and striking pictures beat ordinary ones (up to 10,000 pictures). — https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1973-standing.pdf §Abstract — "The capacity of recognition memory for pictures is almost limitless, when measured under appropriate conditions"

> "Vivid" meant an interesting subject, not technical polish: a dog holding a pipe, a crashed plane. — same URL §Method — "slides with definitely interesting subject matter (with or without technical excellence) were placed in this population, which will be referred to as Vivid pictures."

> Logos: natural (representational, organic) and repeated elements raised correct recognition, in both the US and Chile. — https://estudiosdeadministracion.uchile.cl/index.php/EDA/article/download/56416/59759 §Chile vs US results — "correct recognition can be influenced by natural and repetitive designs in both the US and in Chile."

### Typography

> Good typography reduced frowning and improved creative thinking, even where reading speed did not change. — https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Larson-Hazlett-Chaparro-Picard-2006-measuring-the-aesthetics-of-reading.pdf §Abstract — "improved performance on creative cognitive tasks after text is optimized, and reduced activation in the corrugator muscle that is associated with frowning."

> Readers are 35% faster in their best font than their worst, and the best font differs by person. — https://readabilitymatters.org/articles/towards-individuated-reading-experiences — "Participants' reading speeds (measured in WPM) increased by 35% when comparing fastest and slowest fonts without affecting reading comprehension."

> Fluent reading holds across x-heights of about 0.2° to 2° (4 to 40 point at 40 cm); real books and papers sit inside it. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=21828237&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract — "The fluent range extends over a factor of 10 in angular print size (x-height) from approximately 0.2° to 2°."

> On screens, lines up to 100 characters read faster, but people prefer moderate lines (50–70). Preference and speed disagree. — https://stu.westga.edu/~ssynan1/literacy/Dyson.pdf §Synthesis — "Although longer line lengths may be read faster, people prefer a more moderate length."

> Print tradition: about 52 characters per line was optimal (Tinker, via Rayner & Pollatsek). — same URL — "They deduce that Tinker's work identiﬁed an optimal line length of 52 characters per line."

> "Hard-to-read fonts help memory" failed: pooled effect almost zero (25 articles, 3,135 people). — https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC7854329/fullTextXML §Introduction (reanalysis of Xie 2018) — "the pooled effect size for disfluency on RECALL and TRANSFER performance was almost null."

### Order and grouping

> Grouping by spacing follows a law: its strength falls off exponentially with relative distance ("pure distance law"). — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8142711/ §Introduction (Kubovy & Wagemans 1995) — "the relative strength of grouping dots of a particular orientation approximates a decreasing exponential function of the relative distance to the shortest distance between dots"

> A shared region (box, panel, card) groups harder than being close or looking alike. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=1516361&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract (Palmer 1992) — "this factor strongly influences perceived grouping and is capable of overcoming the effects of other powerful grouping factors such as proximity and similarity."

> Symmetry is detected fast. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=18828191,17016544,12509172,21223945,4655540,15040549&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract (Wagemans 1997) — "Symmetry is a salient visual property that is detected efficiently and rapidly by the human visual system."

> Symmetry was the top predictor of beauty for abstract graphic patterns; complexity came second. — same URL §Abstract (Jacobsen & Höfel 2002) — "As predicted, symmetry had the highest correlations with aesthetic judgments of beauty. Stimulus complexity was the second-highest correlate."

> Vision is limited by spacing between objects more than by their size ("crowding"). — same URL §Abstract (Pelli & Tillman 2008) — "It is now emerging that vision is usually limited by object spacing rather than size."

> Clutter causes crowding, masking, and slow search. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=18217832&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract (Rosenholtz 2007) — "excess and/or disorganized display items can cause crowding, masking, decreased recognition performance due to occlusion, greater difficulty at both segmenting a scene and performing visual search"

> 2026: layouts optimized for grid alignment, grouping, color harmony, and white space beat random and simple-rule layouts in user ratings. — https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03918 §Abstract — "participants rated layouts generated with the full ASP model higher than both random designs and those based on simple heuristics."

> Margins helped reading and satisfaction; line spacing (leading) changed preference only. — https://portfolio.erau.edu/en/publications/reading-online-text-a-comparison-of-four-white-space-layouts/ §Abstract (Chaparro 2004) — "Participants were also generally more satisfied with the text with margins. Leading was not shown to impact reading performance but did influence overall user preference."

> Nuance: the most appealing homepages in one study were dense and bold. — https://humanfactors.com/newsletters/an_exploration_of_relations_between_visual_appeal.asp (Lindgaard 2011) — "high density, high contrast, and very high use of graphics. Further, the graphics tended to use highly saturated colors."

> Experts and non-experts agreed on beauty; typical compositions were rated more beautiful. — https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11176375/fullTextXML §Results (2024) — "typical pictures, regardless of their type, were rated as more beautiful (M = 50.6, SD = 12) than atypical ones (M = 35.1, SD = 8.58)"

### Hierarchy and attention

> Accuracy of reading values, best to worst: position, length and angle, area, volume, then shading and color saturation. — https://math.pku.edu.cn/teachers/xirb/Courses/biostatistics/Biostatistics2016/GraphicalPerception_Jasa1984.pdf §3 Ordering (Cleveland & McGill 1984) — "1. Position along a common scale 2. Positions along nonaligned scales 3. Length, direction, angle 4. Area 5. Volume, curvature 6. Shading, color saturation"

> Faces grab the eye: over 80% of viewers look at a face within two fixations. — https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2007/hash/708f3cf8100d5e71834b1db77dfa15d6-Abstract.html §Abstract (Cerf 2008) — "fixate on a face with a probability of over 80% within their first two fixations (500ms)."

> Text grabs the eye through its visual form, beyond ordinary salience. — https://arg-nctu.github.io/publications/WangPomplun2012.pdf §Abstract — "specific visual features of texts, rather than typically salient features (e.g., color, orientation, and contrast), are the main attractors of attention"

> A face looking toward the message lifts attention to it and memory for it. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=24624104&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract — "the condition involving faces with averted gaze increased attention to the banner overall, as well as to the advertising text and product."

> A face staring at the viewer steals attention from the message. — same URL — "in the condition involving faces with mutual gaze, the focus of attention was localized more on the face region rather than on the text or product"

> Web readers scan an F: lead with information-carrying words (232 users, industry study). — https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/ — "Start subheads, paragraphs, and bullet points with information-carrying words that users will notice when scanning down the left side of your content"

### Contrast, fit, and belief

> Text contrast 4.5:1 compensates for about 20/40 vision. — https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html — "The contrast ratio of 4.5:1 was chosen for level AA because it compensated for the loss in contrast sensitivity usually experienced by users with vision loss equivalent to approximately 20/40 vision."

> Easy-to-read statements are believed more. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=10487787&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract (Reber & Schwarz 1999) — "Moderately visible statements were judged as true at chance level, whereas highly visible statements were judged as true significantly above chance level."

> A fitting font doubled brand choice. — https://www.statsclass.org/stat110/notes/Font_Appropriateness_and_Brand_Choice.pdf §Abstract (Doyle & Bottomley 2004) — "brands, covering 10 different product categories, were chosen twice as frequently when they were in an appropriate font relative to when they were not."

> Color–emotion links are mostly shared worldwide (30 nations, 4,598 people). — https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/169496/ §Abstract — "Pattern-similarity analyses revealed universal color-emotion associations (average similarity coefficient r = .88)."

### More on memory

> One item that differs from similar neighbors is remembered better (isolation effect). — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=24203592&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract (Hunt 1995) — "If all but one item of a list are similar on some dimension, memory for the different item will be enhanced."

> The difference only counts against a background of similarity. — same URL — "the difference between the isolated and surrounding items is not sufficient to produce isolation effects but must be considered in the context of similarity."
