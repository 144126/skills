# Pleasing video edits: root research

Question: what makes a video edit feel good, peeled to first principles, for Claude to use when making edits.

Status: done (45 search turns, 38 sources). Deeper aesthetics of motion, color, and shape: [aesthetics-motion-color-shape.md](aesthetics-motion-color-shape.md). Same floor as [creativity-first-principles.md](https://github.com/144126/skills/blob/master/skills/creative/references/creativity-first-principles.md).

The rule I follow: [video-edit](https://github.com/144126/skills/blob/master/skills/video-edit/SKILL.md).

## Floor

The viewer's brain predicts the next moment. It splits what it sees into events at the points where its predictions fail.

An edit feels good in two ways:
- **Hidden:** the cut lands where the brain already expects a change (the end of an action, a beat, an exit from frame). The cut disappears into a gap the brain was about to make anyway.
- **Felt:** the cut breaks a prediction, and the very next moment explains it (a match cut, a drop, a surprise). Surprising, then inevitable.

Rhythm pleases the same way: mostly predictable, with some breaks.

## Layers peeled (shortest first)

1. Cut on action and on the beat. Keep sound running across the cut. Vary shot length.
2. Why can a cut vanish? Vision is already full of gaps (eye jumps and blinks blank it). Attention on motion and story hides the cut. Sound glues shots together.
3. Why cut at action changes and beats? The brain marks new events where predictions fail. Viewers blink together at moments with little information. Beats set up the moments where change is expected.
4. Why guide the eyes? After a cut, eyes jump to the center and to motion. Film controls gaze strongly. If the focus lands where the eyes already are, there is no search cost.
5. Why rhythm and variety? Attention itself swings on slow and fast timescales (1/f, a mix of both). Medium syncopation (some off-beat hits) pleases most. Wanting builds during the lead-up; liking comes at the peak.
6. Why does meaning jump across cuts? The brain links neighbouring shots through action, gaze, and sound, and colours one shot with the other (Kuleshov).
7. Next "why" (why does the brain predict at all?) is a different question. Stop.

## Where the evidence is thin

- "Overlap the action about 2 frames" is editors' practice reported in a thesis, not a test.
- Beat-cut timing uses the TV lip-sync limits. Nobody I found tested those limits on music cuts directly.
- No direct study of match-cut beauty was found. Only a definition from Netflix research.
- Short-form stats ("cut every 1.5–3 s", "jump cuts raise completion 26%") came only from marketing blogs. Left out.

## Lookalikes split

- **Invisible cut vs felt cut.** A continuity cut should not be noticed. A jump, smash, or match cut is meant to be felt.
- **Where vs what vs how long vs how joined.** Cut point (timing), shot choice and order, shot length (pace), and the join (cut, dissolve, sound bridge) are different decisions.
- **Pleasing vs understood vs watched.** Liking, understanding, and staying to watch are different results. An edit can win one and lose another.

## Evidence (pinned)

### Cuts can hide inside how we already see

> Viewers split a film into events mostly where the action changes, much less where place or time jumps. — https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/sites.wustl.edu/dist/e/952/files/2017/09/maglianoandzacks2011-22vhbrv.pdf §Abstract — "Discontinuities in action had the biggest impact on behavioral event segmentation, and discontinuities in space and time had minor effects."

> Editing is built so that meaning bridges the visual break. — same URL §Abstract — "commercial film editing is shaped to support the comprehension of meaningful events that bridge breaks in low-level visual continuity"

> Continuity works when the cut does not grab attention and things show up where and when expected. — https://era.ed.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/487e8149-f89a-4ded-82f7-ac0a8cfaa274/content §Abstract (Smith PhD 2005) — "continuity editing ensures existence constancy by creating conditions under which a) the visual disruption created by the cut does not capture attention"

> After the cut, the object only has to appear when and where expected. — same URL §Abstract — "After the cut, the object only has to appear when and where it is expected for it to be perceived as continuing to exist."

> Motion after the cut hides it. Motion before the cut matters less. — https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/14904/1/CutDetect_MediaPsych_timjsmith_unblind_preprint.pdf §Abstract — "Removing post-cut motion significantly decreased cut detection time and the probability of missing the cut."

> Sound hides cuts: without the soundtrack, edit blindness mostly vanished. — same URL §Abstract — "Audio played a surprisingly large part in creating edit blindness with edit blindness mostly disappearing without audio."

> Editor's rule (not tested in that thesis): overlap the action about 2 frames at a match cut. — same thesis URL §Chapter 6 — "the action (not the actual film) should be overlapped approximately two frames when making the splice"

### Viewers share natural pauses

> Viewers of the same video blink together (14 people analysed). — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2817301/ §Abstract — "spontaneous blinks were highly synchronized between and within subjects when they viewed the same short video stories"

> They blink when little is happening. — same URL §Abstract — "Synchronized blinks occurred during scenes that required less attention such as at the conclusion of an action, during the absence of the main character, during a long shot"

> The brain times blinks to lose the least. — same URL §Abstract — "an implicit timing that is appropriate to minimize the chance of losing critical information while viewing a stream of visual events"

### Rhythm of shot lengths

> Over 70 years (150 films), shot lengths drifted toward the 1/f pattern of human attention. — https://jordandelong.com/pubs/2010/AttentionEvolution.pdf §Abstract — "shots became increasingly more correlated in length with their neighbors and created power spectra approaching 1/f."

> Editors set the rhythm of attention. — same URL §Introduction — "the rhythm of shot sequences in film is designed to drive the rhythm of attention and information uptake in the viewer."

> Not just fast: all-short shots is not the aim. — same URL §Discussion — "Otherwise, all films would be composed of unremittingly short shots."

### The deepest layer found: prediction error marks events

> The brain predicts what comes next. When the prediction fails, it marks a new event. — https://bpb-us-e2.wpmucdn.com/sites.wustl.edu/dist/e/952/files/2017/09/zackspsychbull07-sbm4za.pdf §Abstract — "Perceptual systems continuously make predictions about what will happen next. When transient errors in predictions arise, an event boundary is perceived."

### Edits steer eyes and brains

> Motion predicts where everyone looks, and when all eyes gather on one spot. — https://homepages.inf.ed.ac.uk/rhill2/pubs/Mital_(2011)_Clustering_of_gaze_during_dynamic_scene_viewing.pdf §Abstract — "moments in which all viewers' gaze tightly clustered in the same location could be predicted by motion."

> Right after a cut, eyes jump to screen center. — same URL §Introduction — "a tendency for viewers to saccade to the screen center immediately following scene onset (or following a cut)"

> Viewers look where the film makes them look, almost regardless of what they understand. — https://www.k-state.edu/psych/vcl/applied-research/Loschky_Larson_Magliano_Smith_2015_What_would_Jaws_do.pdf §Abstract — "overall, the results were more consistent with the tyranny of film hypothesis than the mental model hypothesis."

> Edited film lines up viewers' brains; one unedited shot of a park barely does (Hitchcock over 65% of cortex vs under 5%). — https://www.motionpictures.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/01/Hasson-etal_NeuroCinematics2008.pdf §Results — "The Hitchcock episode (Figure 7, green) evoked similar responses across all viewers in over 65 percent of the cortex"

> Films got quicker, more full of motion, and darker, to steer attention (160 films, 1935–2010). — https://jordandelong.com/pubs/2011/QuickerFasterDarker.pdf §Abstract — "Filmmakers have incrementally tried to exercise more control over the attention of filmgoers."

> Average shot length fell from about 10 s to under 4 s. — same URL §Results — "with ASLs of about 10 s in the 1930s and 1940s falling to below 4 s"

### What a cut means comes from what joins it

> Kuleshov effect (a blank face "shows" the feeling of the shot next to it): real, but modest (36 people). — https://www.lunduniversity.lu.se/publication/ca3d37a0-82b5-487e-a523-188ff1564d0a — searched page summary; claim from Perception 2016 abstract: participants "tended to choose the appropriate category more frequently than the alternative options"

> 2026: context shifts how good or bad a face looks, not which emotion it shows (32 people). — https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:41815739%20AND%20SRC:MED&resultType=core&format=json §Abstract — "positive contexts led to higher facial valence ratings, while negative contexts led to lower ones"

> First-time film viewers link shots through a familiar action. — https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/21143/2/6db4c380-e9a9-4dcb-a7ca-817518f75d28.pdf §Abstract — "often had difficulty integrating shots into a coherent representation of the depicted events in the absence of a familiar action through the film cuts"

> Sound bridges shots, even for first-time viewers. — same URL §Abstract — "both dialog, and salient environmental sound (e.g., barking dogs) helped first-time viewers connect shots."

### Sound and picture timing

> TV standard: sound early by more than 45 ms is noticed; sound late up to 125 ms is not. — https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bt/R-REC-BT.1359-1-199811-I!!PDF-E.pdf §considering g) — "subjective evaluations show that detectability thresholds are about +45 ms to –125 ms and acceptability thresholds are about +90 ms to –185 ms on the average, a positive value indicates that sound is advanced"

> Film target: within half a frame. — same URL §Appendix 1 — "should be within ± half a frame. For 24 fps film, this is an acceptable variation of about ±22 ms."

### Rhythm

> Drum grooves: medium syncopation (off-beat hits) gives the most pleasure and urge to move (66 people, 50 drum-breaks). — https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0094446 §Results — "medium degrees of syncopation elicited the most desire to move and the most pleasure"

> Why: enough regularity to predict, enough surprise to break it. — same URL §Discussion — "sufficient rhythmic predictability for metre to be perceived and metrical expectations to occur, and sufficient complexity for those expectations to be violated"

### Dialogue timing

> Conversation gaps are about 200 ms. — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00731/full §Introduction — "gaps between turns are short (of the order of 200 ms)"

> Most turn changes fall between −100 and 500 ms. — same URL — "the great proportion of turn transitions fall between -100 and 500 ms"

> The pattern holds across 10 languages. — https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1073/pnas.0903616106&resultType=core&format=json §Abstract (Stivers 2009) — "a general avoidance of overlapping talk and a minimization of silence between conversational turns"

### Beat sync

> Cuts on the music beat raise pleasure, even when viewers cannot tell. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=35398533&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract (Neuroscience Letters 2022) — "Despite the subliminal nature of this manipulation, a higher perceptual pleasure was found for temporal congruency compared with incongruency."

> "Visual beats" are the turning points of motion. Lining them up with musical beats looks like dance. — https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_cvpr_2018_workshops/papers/w49/Davis_Visual_Rhythm_and_CVPR_2018_paper.pdf §Abstract — "alignment of visual rhythm with its musical counterpart results in the appearance of dance."

> Where the visual beats sit: the high and low points of a gesture. — same URL §Figure 1 — "These visual beats lie at the high and low points of a repetitive up-and-down hand gesture."

> Listeners' blinks lock to the beat, one to two beats apart (2025). — https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.3003456 §Results — "the inter-blink interval is most often one to two beats"

> Pleasure has two phases: wanting during the build-up, and liking at the peak. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=21217764&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract (Salimpoor 2011) — "the caudate was more involved during the anticipation and the nucleus accumbens was more involved during the experience of peak emotional responses to music."

### Pace and structure

> Faster cutting makes time feel faster (39 people). — https://labocinemedias.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/The_Effect_of_Music_and_Editin.pdf §Abstract — "Fast-paced editing was associated with the feeling of time passing faster, a relationship mediated by fixation durations."

> Blockbusters: shots get much shorter in the first part of the climax. — https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC5133278/fullTextXML §Results (Cutting 2016) — "Shot durations fall precipitously during the first part of the climax. This is where physical action is typically greatest."

> Then they lengthen again in the epilog. — same URL — "they rise smartly during the epilog, when diegetic world order is generally restored"

> Scenes open wide, then move in to faces. — same URL — "Scenes tend to begin with a longer-scale shot and become shorter as the camera moves in to show more of the faces of the characters"

### Dialogue and talking heads

> Vlog jump cuts feel smooth because speech flows across them and lips match sound. — https://firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/download/10547/10070 — "the two sensory modes of sight and sound are in simultaneous agreeance before and after the jump cut"

> Computer editing rules from film practice: avoid jump cuts between two shots of the same speaker; do not cut away for a very short line. — https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/roughcut/files/roughcut-small.pdf §Table 1 — "Avoid cutting to a new shot for only a short amount of time to prevent rapid, successive cuts that can be jarring."

> Close-ups for emotional lines. — same URL §Table 1 — "Use close ups for particularly emotional lines to provide more detail in the performer's face."

> Captions: viewers keep up with fast subtitles; speed from 12 to 20 characters per second did not hurt understanding (74 people). — https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0199331 §Results — "Subtitle speed did not have an effect on comprehension"

### Breaking the rules

> Breaking the 180-degree rule confuses viewers but did not lower liking. — https://pure.hud.ac.uk/ws/files/16045791/Kachkovski_17_180Rule_OA.pdf §Abstract — "violations did not alter the viewers' liking of a video: Viewers were as likely to prefer a video with a 180-degree violation as one without."

> Every cut registers in the brain like a grammar break; rule-breaking cuts differ later, in remapping space. — https://sas-space.sas.ac.uk/6561/1/Heimann%20et%20al%202016.pdf §Abstract — "cuts and edits in general elicit early ERP component indicating the registration of syntactic violations as known from language, music, and action processing."

### More: exits, attention, curiosity, speech

> Cut the moment a moving subject fully leaves the frame: the eye jumps ahead, and the jump hides the cut. — https://era.ed.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/487e8149-f89a-4ded-82f7-ac0a8cfaa274/content §4.5 Experiment 1: Conclusion — "subjects are only able to time a saccadic eye movement to coincide with a cut when the cut occurs as the focal object is fully occluded by the screen edge (100% Exit)."

> Subject fully in view right after the cut was rated smoothest. — same URL §4.5 — "Viewer's rating of cuts with 100% Entry as creating the "smoothest" impression of motion"

> The start of motion grabs attention; ongoing motion much less. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=12930472,19619181&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract (Abrams 2003) — "There was no advantage for moving letters among static ones, but there was an advantage for objects that had recently started to move"

> Each eye jump dims vision for a moment. That is one gap a cut can hide in. — https://www.pisavisionlab.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/annurev-vision-091517-034317-1.pdf §Abstract — "Each large saccade is accompanied by a drop of sensitivity to luminance-contrast, low-frequency stimuli"

> Classic film finding: looking rose with cutting rate and with how complex the shots were. — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-018-0111-y (review of Hochberg and Brooks) — "Looking preference increased with cutting rate and with complexity of shot contents."

> Time to look is dosed by novelty and complexity. — same URL — "The reward of comprehension is carefully dosed by varying the time allowed to the viewer to inspect objects and scenes, dependent on their novelty and complexity."

> Curiosity peaks when people are about 50% sure (19 people in the scanner). — https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/9f1n0-x0z52/files/ssrn-id1308286_1_.pdf?download=1 §Results — "Reported curiosity is indeed an inverted U-shaped function of P, with maximum curiosity when P is around 50%"

> In online video ads, surprise and joy hold attention and stop skipping. Rising joy matters more than the level of joy. — http://www.economicsofattention.com/site/assets/files/1054/emotion-induced_engagement-_teixeira_et_al-_jmr.pdf §Abstract — "the level rather than the velocity of surprise affects attention concentration most, whereas the velocity rather than the level of joy affects viewer retention most."

> Ads that keep everyone's eyes on one small area get skipped less. — same URL §Introduction — "television advertisements that concentrate viewers' visual attention in a small region of the screen, from moment to moment, rather than allow their attention to freely wander and disperse across the screen, retained viewers longer and were zapped less"

> A silent pause before a word makes it better remembered. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=20950633&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract — "listeners were more likely to recognise words which had been encountered after silent pauses"

> Match cuts: same framing of the subject, or the same movement across the cut. — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.05766 §Introduction — "character frame match cuts, in which the framing of the character in the first shot aligns with the character in the second shot, and (2) motion match cuts"

> A camera that moves toward the scene feels more involving than a static one. — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=24666130&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract — "Videos in which the camera approached the scene were felt as more involving"

> One cut inside a 2–3 s clip makes it feel longer (90 + 60 people). — https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=36418366&rettype=abstract&retmode=text §Abstract — "scenes containing continuous editing were perceived as longer than the other two scene types."
